# The Field-Marketing Ops Kit

*How one marketing director ran ~35 event workstreams across two markets in 90 days, and where the agent ends and the marketer begins.*

Field marketing is the most judgment-dense discipline in B2B: every event is a bet placed with real money on the right room, the right format, and the right follow-through, and most of the work that decides the outcome is unglamorous coverage: knowing what's happening in your markets and hitting unforgiving external deadlines, then extracting the lessons before they evaporate. This kit is the operating machine I built for that work: one continuous workflow from pre-event radar to post-event ROI review, with the agent running the coverage and me placing the bets.

The numbers first, since this is a playbook and playbooks should carry receipts: in one 90-day window this system handled roughly 35 distinct event workstreams (scans, go/no-go research calls, two full event executions, VIP programs, and retrospectives) across Southeast Asia and China. Counts come from the system's own logs and carry their trust labels in the [outcomes ledger](outcomes.md); every name is stripped; the methods are exact.

## 1. Radar: never hear about an event from a customer

A recurring automated scan sweeps the sources where events in our markets surface (industry calendars, chamber-of-commerce feeds, partner announcements, competitor activity) against a keyword frame I approved (the methodology is a reviewed document, not a prompt: audience dimensions, topic dimensions, region tiers). Qualified hits land in a shared calendar with structured fields:

| Field | Why it exists |
|-------|---------------|
| Date, city, format | The logistics floor |
| Audience: who is actually in the room | The only field that decides anything |
| Relevance rationale | One paragraph arguing why we'd care, so I can disagree with the *reasoning*, not just the verdict |
| Decision needed | attend / speak / sponsor / skip — pre-framed so my answer is one word |

Two radar features earn their keep more than discovery itself:

**Triage at hostile volume.** For one major industry week, the side-event list ran past a hundred entries. The agent triaged the full list against our audience frame and returned a top five with reasoning per pick and an explicit pass-tier ("worth it only if X falls through"). I spot-checked the reasoning, overrode one pick, and the week was planned in a sitting. The deliverable is never "here's everything I found"; it's "here's the decision, pre-chewed, with my work shown."

**Clash detection.** When several relevant events land in the same week across different cities, the radar surfaces the collision as its own alert ("three cities, one week: who goes where?") instead of letting it be discovered in week-of panic. Sequencing conflicts are the most expensive kind to find late, because the fix, splitting seniority across cities, needs lead time.

## 2. The go/no-go layer: where the marketer is unautomatable

Radar output is input. The calls are mine, and the calls are the job:

- **Audience math before brand math.** One city's program came in scoped as an open summit. The audience research didn't support brand-level spend, so I repositioned it as a closed-door sales event: smaller room, a single north-star metric (signed commitments, not attendance), and a budget cut to match. The agent re-planned the execution to the new premise within the day. Repositioning calls like that decide more of an event's return than everything downstream combined, and no part of this one was automated.
- **Every event carries one north-star metric and a budget it will be judged against,** both set at go/no-go time, not after. An event that can't name its metric doesn't get budget.
- **Skip is a decision, not an absence.** Pass verdicts get recorded with reasons, so next year's radar hit doesn't relitigate them from zero.

## 3. Execution: guards, scripts, and scoring at intake

Once an event is live, the agent carries the production floor:

**Deadline guards.** Event deliverables have unforgiving external deadlines: booth artwork to printers, speaker forms, registration cutoffs. Each gets a guard task: the artifact ships early, then on deadline day the guard checks whether the stakeholder responded. If yes, act on the feedback; if silent, exactly one nudge, fallback attached: "deadline is today; the print-ready fallback is in your DM; say the word or I submit." A guard never re-produces delivered work and never nags twice.

**Run-of-show as a deliverable.** MC scripts, demo-zone layouts, staffing grids, all produced to the same taste gate as published content, because a stumbling MC script is brand damage with a microphone.

**Fit scoring at intake.** Registration forms score attendees against our ideal-customer profile *as they register*, so by event day the follow-up queue is already prioritized. The window for post-event follow-up is short and the highest-fit conversations should not wait for someone to sort a spreadsheet after the booth is packed.

## 4. The AAR protocol: retrospectives that chase their own data

Field marketing's dirtiest secret is that most event learnings die in the two weeks after the event, because retrospectives depend on busy people filling in forms. The protocol here inverts the labor:

1. **Evidence sweep first.** Before any human is asked anything, the agent sweeps the event's own exhaust (for one flagship event, roughly two thousand messages across four working channels) and compiles a structured evidence document: in that case, 21 distinct problems and 12 highlights, each with source quotes. The retro starts from evidence, not from memory.
2. **Three layers, pre-filled.** The retro template separates what happened (facts, pre-filled from records), why it happened (analysis, drafted from evidence for humans to correct), and what we change (decisions, humans only). People supply only what only they know.
3. **Open-ended where judgment is the payload.** The questionnaire asks yes/no about logistics and open questions about everything that matters. A checklist verifies what you can predict; a retro exists to collect what you can't. This distinction was a correction I made once, at format level, and it now governs every instrument the system designs.
4. **The chase ladder.** Named owners get polite escalation on a schedule (day 3, day 8, day 19). Items still unfilled past the ladder surface as explicit close-or-kill cards ("fill these five fields or say archive") so nothing rots silently as fake pending work.

5. **The AAR closes the commercial loop.** Every retro ends by scoring the event against the numbers set at go/no-go: actual spend against budget, actual result against the north-star metric. Met expectations or didn't, and if it didn't, the miss gets named and priced into the next go/no-go. Marketing that can't say what an event returned against what it cost isn't a function; it's a cost center with catering.

The output compounds: every AAR feeds the go/no-go layer, so next quarter's bets are placed against last quarter's evidence instead of last quarter's impressions.

## A worked shape: two cities, ninety days

One program window, anonymized to its shape: a flagship summit in one market and a repositioned closed-door event in another, run concurrently by one director. Radar and clash detection set the calendar; go/no-go calls set format and metric per city; guards carried both production floors in parallel; both events got evidence-swept AARs, and one AAR finding (an intake-flow failure) became a permanent registration-design rule the following month. Total incremental headcount: zero. The scarce input, start to finish, was my judgment.

## What stays human

Worth naming explicitly, because the honest boundary is the credibility of everything above: relationships and room-reading (the conversations that make a VIP dinner worth its budget), negotiation with venues and partners, every go/no-go and repositioning call, and the political judgment of who from the company belongs in which room. The agent has never made any of those calls and isn't designed to. It made them *cheaper to make well*, by ensuring that when I make them, the evidence is already compiled and nothing downstream drops.
